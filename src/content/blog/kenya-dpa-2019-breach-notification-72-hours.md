Most breach-notification conversations we have with clients start the same way: "we'll notify once we know what happened." Under Kenya's Data Protection Act 2019, that instinct is the single most common way a manageable incident becomes a regulatory one.

## The clock starts on awareness, not resolution

Section 43 requires notification to the Office of the Data Protection Commissioner within **72 hours of the controller becoming aware** that a breach has occurred — not 72 hours after root cause is confirmed, not after the incident is contained, and not after a full forensic report is ready. "Aware" is a lower bar than most engineering teams assume: a credible alert from a monitoring system, a customer report of unauthorized access, or a vendor disclosure can all start the clock.

We've reviewed incident timelines where the technical response was genuinely fast — systems isolated within hours, forensics underway by end of day — but the notification was filed on day five because the team was waiting for certainty. Certainty is not the standard. Awareness is.

## What "notification" actually needs to contain

A DPA-compliant initial notification doesn't need a finished narrative. It needs:

- The nature of the breach, in as much detail as is known at the time
- The categories and approximate number of data subjects affected
- The likely consequences of the breach
- The measures taken or proposed to address it

A preliminary filing that gets amended as facts firm up is compliant. A silent five-day investigation followed by a single polished report is not — regardless of how good the report is.

## Where this actually breaks in practice

In our incident response engagements across East African fintech and healthcare clients, the gap is almost never technical. It's organizational: nobody on the team has explicit authority to trigger the 72-hour clock without a chain of internal sign-offs that itself takes three days.

If your incident response plan has a notification decision gated behind more than one internal approval layer, that plan will fail the DPA's timeline the first time it's tested for real. We build this as a named, single-owner decision point in every DR runbook we deliver — because the alternative is discovering the gap during an actual incident, in front of a regulator.
