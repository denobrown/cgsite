We ask every new client the same question during an ISO 27001 readiness review: "What does your scope statement actually say?" More often than we'd like, the honest answer is that nobody in the room has read it recently — or at all.

## Certification is scoped, not company-wide

An ISO 27001 certificate doesn't certify "the company." It certifies the Information Security Management System (ISMS) as defined by a specific scope statement — which might cover a particular data center, a specific product line, or a named set of business processes, and explicitly exclude everything else. That's not a loophole; it's how the standard is designed to work, and it's entirely legitimate to certify a defined scope rather than an entire organization.

The problem isn't the scoping itself. It's that the scope statement is usually written once, during initial certification, and then never revisited as the business changes — new products launch, new vendors get onboarded, new systems get built — while the certificate keeps renewing on schedule without anyone checking whether the scope still matches reality.

## Where this actually bites

We've reviewed post-incident timelines where the technical response was genuinely competent, and the client's first instinct — understandably — was to point to their ISO 27001 certificate as evidence of a mature security posture. Then someone checks the scope statement, and the system that was actually compromised was a newer product line launched eighteen months after the original certification scope was defined, and never added to it.

This isn't a hypothetical edge case. It's the single most common gap we find between "what a client believes their certification covers" and "what it actually covers on paper," and it surfaces at the worst possible time — during an incident, in front of a client, a regulator, or an insurer, none of whom will accept "we assumed it was covered" as an answer.

## What to actually check, and how often

Pull your current Statement of Applicability and scope statement, and compare them against your actual current architecture — not your architecture from the last audit cycle. Specifically:

- New products or business units launched since the scope was last defined
- New third-party processors or cloud providers now handling in-scope data
- Systems that were explicitly out of scope at certification time but have since started handling data that would put them in scope if evaluated today

We recommend doing this review at least annually, and explicitly — not as a side effect of the surveillance audit cycle, which checks conformance to the *existing* scope, not whether the scope itself is still the right one.
